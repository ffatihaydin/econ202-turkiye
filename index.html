02 // Introduction

Where is Türkiye, really?

I picked Türkiye because it is the country I am from and because no other major emerging market has gone through what it has gone through in the last four years. Inflation above 75%, a currency cut roughly in half, a full pivot in monetary policy, and now a slow disinflation that nobody is fully sure will hold.

This project asks three things. Where is Türkiye in its business cycle right now? Is the orthodox monetary policy under TCMB Governor Karahan actually working? And what are the biggest risks to disinflation between now and the next election cycle? I am writing in April 2026 with the most recent data I could get, and I tried to use sources that disagree with each other so the picture is honest, not flattering.

03 // Business Cycle

Soft landing in progress.

Türkiye is in a soft-landing phase. Growth is slowing but still positive, inflation is falling but still high, monetary policy is tight, and the government is doing modest fiscal consolidation. That is a very different picture from 2021 to 2023, when the economy was running hot on negative real interest rates, FX-protected deposits, and a credit boom that pushed inflation past 75% YoY.

The clearest signal is the quarterly growth path. After the overheating of 2021 and the stimulus-fueled 2022, q/q growth flattened and even went slightly negative in mid-2024 before turning back up. By Q4 2025 the q/q rate was 0.4%, which is exactly the kind of modest expansion you want to see while the central bank is still trying to drag inflation down.

Source: TÜİK quarterly national accounts, BBVA Research Türkiye Outlook, March 2026.
04 // GDP & Growth

Growth is back, but the mix is wrong.

Real GDP grew 3.6% in 2025, according to BBVA Research. Q4 2025 q/q was 0.4%, and the World Bank projects 3.7% in 2026 and 4.4% in 2027. GDP per capita is around $19,018 in 2026 according to the IMF, which puts Türkiye solidly in the upper-middle income group.

The problem is composition. Growth is still mostly domestic-demand heavy. Consumption and construction are doing the work. Net exports are a drag, partly because the lira appreciated in real terms during the disinflation period and partly because external demand from Europe is weak. The whole point of the rebalancing program was to shift the mix toward exports and investment, and that part has not really happened yet.

05 // Price Level

Disinflation is real. It is also slow and bumpy.

This is the headline story of the Turkish economy. CPI peaked above 75% YoY in May 2024, then fell hard through the rest of 2024 and most of 2025. The latest official print from TÜİK is 30.87% YoY in March 2026, down from 31.53% in February. Core inflation came in at 29.68%.

That is real progress. It is also worth saying out loud that the independent ENAG group reports 54.62% for the same month, and that gap has become its own story about data trust. Even taking the official series at face value, housing inflation is still around 42% and education is above 50%, so the disinflation is unevenly distributed across the basket.

The path from here is not a straight line. February actually ticked up before March came back down, and that kind of noise is going to keep happening as long as services inflation stays sticky and the lira is sensitive to geopolitics.

Source: TÜİK monthly CPI release, March 2026. Dashed line is the TCMB 5% medium-term target.
06 // Labor Markets

The cooling shows up here first.

Unemployment was 8.5% in February 2026, up from a record low of 7.7% in December 2025. That uptick is what a soft landing looks like, not a recession yet. Tight monetary policy is supposed to cool labor markets, and a small move from 7.7 to 8.5 is closer to mission accomplished than to alarm.

The structural numbers are the bigger problem. Youth unemployment is 15.8%, female unemployment is 11.6% versus male at 6.9%, and labor force participation is only 52.6%, which is low by OECD standards. Disinflation will not fix any of that. Those are education, childcare, and labor regulation problems that need their own policy track.

Source: TÜİK Labour Force Statistics, monthly release through February 2026.
07 // International Trade

The deficit is widening again.

The trade deficit widened to $11.3B in March 2026 per the Trade Ministry, and the Q1 2026 deficit was $28.7B against $22.5B in Q1 2025. Top export destinations are Germany, the US, Italy, the UK, and France. Top import sources are China, Germany, Russia, Switzerland, and the US.

The current account deficit hit $7.5B in February 2026, the largest monthly print since April 2025. Tourism receipts and services trade keep the headline current account manageable, but the underlying goods deficit is the part that scares analysts. Real lira appreciation during the disinflation period is making Turkish exports less price-competitive, which shows up directly in this widening gap.

Source: Türkiye Trade Ministry monthly trade balance, January 2024 to March 2026.
08 // Economic Threats

What could still break the story.

Three risks, in the order I rank them. The point of this section is not to be alarmist, it is to flag what would actually move the disinflation path off course.

1

Inflation re-acceleration from energy shocks

BBVA Research flags higher energy prices and reserve depletion as the top external risks. Türkiye imports roughly all of its oil and gas. Any sustained Middle East tension that pushes Brent above $95 would feed straight into headline CPI within two months and unwind a chunk of the 2024 to 2025 disinflation.

Severity
2

Political pressure on the central bank

The orthodox stance under Governor Karahan is institutionally fragile. There is precedent for governor turnover and rate cuts that markets did not expect. Any reversal here would crash the lira and erase the credibility that is doing most of the disinflation work right now.

Severity
3

External vulnerability

FX reserves are still thin once you net out swap lines and gold. The current account deficit is widening. Gross external debt is high. None of this is fatal alone, but it leaves Türkiye dependent on portfolio inflows that can stop suddenly if global risk appetite turns.

Severity
09 // Fiscal Policy

Mildly contractionary, but the headline is misleading.

Government debt to GDP was 23.8% at the end of December 2025 according to Treasury data via CEIC. That number sounds fantastic. By rich-country standards it basically is. The headline hides earthquake reconstruction spending, rising interest costs that are expected to consume 15 to 20% of the 2026 budget, and a steady stream of off-budget items that do not show up in the debt ratio.

The 2026 budget targets a deficit around 3.4% of GDP, but January 2026 already showed a 246B TL deficit on its own. The good news came in February, when the primary balance flipped to a 208.1B TL surplus versus a 170.4B TL deficit a year earlier. So the fiscal stance is consolidating, just slowly and with a lot of month-to-month volatility.

Source: Türkiye Ministry of Treasury and Finance, OECD fiscal database, IMF Article IV. 2026 forecast.
10 // Monetary Policy

TCMB held at 37%.

TCMB held the policy rate at 37% at the April 2026 meeting, after cutting from a 50% peak through five consecutive meetings. Overnight lending is at 40%, overnight borrowing at 35.5%. The bank reiterated that tight policy will continue until the 5% medium-term inflation target is achieved.

The bank also intervened to defend the lira in March 2026 after geopolitical tensions pushed the currency lower. That tells you the framework is working through three channels at once. The demand channel cools consumption and credit. The exchange rate channel imports lower prices through a stable lira. The expectations channel anchors wage and price-setting behavior, which matters most in a country where households are still scarred by the 2022 to 2024 inflation spike.

The hold at 37% rather than another cut was the right call. Cutting into a February CPI re-acceleration would have undone a lot of credibility for not much real-rate relief.

Source: TCMB monetary policy decisions, January 2023 through April 2026.
11 // Fiscal Recommendations

What I would do on the fiscal side.

These are my recommendations as a student, not policy advice. Each is meant to support the disinflation program rather than fight it.

Recommendation 01

Lock in the primary surplus.

February 2026 already showed a 208.1B TL primary surplus. The path should be a binding rule that pushes the primary balance toward 1% of GDP by 2027. Without that anchor, every electoral cycle becomes a fight over spending and the bond market prices in a risk premium that the 23.8% debt ratio does not deserve.

Recommendation 02

Phase out FX-protected deposits faster.

The KKM scheme made sense as a crisis tool in 2021. In 2026, with rates at 37% and the lira relatively stable, it is just a contingent liability that grows whenever the currency weakens. Treasury should publish a clear glide path to zero KKM stock by end-2026 and let the central bank take over FX risk management through normal reserves.

Recommendation 03

Broaden the tax base, not just the rate.

Türkiye leans heavily on indirect taxes like VAT and excise. Those are regressive and they show up in headline CPI, which makes the central bank's job harder. The right move is to expand direct taxation, especially property and capital gains, while lowering or stabilizing VAT on essentials. That is better disinflation policy and better distributional policy at the same time.

12 // Monetary Recommendations

What I would do on the monetary side.

The April hold was right. The harder question is what the next six months look like.

Recommendation 01

Do not cut again until CPI is below 25% for two months.

March 2026 came in at 30.87%. Cutting before that number has a clear sub-25% reading would invite the same expectations un-anchoring that 2021 produced. Two consecutive months below 25% is a simple, communicable rule that keeps the bank disciplined without locking it into a date.

Recommendation 02

Keep the real ex-ante policy rate at 5 to 7%.

With core inflation at 29.68% and the policy rate at 37%, the real ex-ante rate is in roughly the right zone if you use 12-month-ahead inflation expectations near 22 to 25%. The bank should not let that real rate fall below 5% during the rest of 2026, even if pressure for cuts intensifies.

Recommendation 03

Build FX reserves while the lira is calm.

March 2026 intervention to support the lira shows reserves are the binding constraint, not the rate. The bank should be a steady net buyer of USD whenever portfolio inflows are positive, even at the cost of a slightly weaker lira. Reserves bought today are insurance against the energy shock or political shock that risks the disinflation story.

13 // Conclusion

So, is it working?

Mostly yes. The orthodox policy turn dragged headline inflation from above 75% to roughly 31% in less than two years. Growth slowed but did not collapse, unemployment ticked up only slightly, and the primary balance is improving. By the standards of an emerging market that was inflating at 75%, this is a real success and it is fair to say Governor Karahan and the Treasury have earned the credibility they currently have.

The questions I cannot answer are political. Will TCMB independence hold through the 2027 election cycle, especially if growth slows further? Will the disinflation curve stay smooth, or will an energy shock or a lira event break it? And what happens to the KKM stock if the lira weakens 15% in a single quarter? I do not think anyone has clean answers to those.

If I extended this project, I would dig into household balance sheets, since the wealth effects of the 2022 to 2024 inflation are still showing up in consumption patterns, and into the regional breakdown of unemployment, since the headline 8.5% hides huge variance between Istanbul-Ankara-Izmir and the southeast. Both feel like the next layer of this story.
